Transit Tales:
make the journey the playground.

Eight feasible, playful directions for elevators, escalators, and metro networks—with the decisions, delightful moments, and small prototypes that could make them real.

These are design concepts, not implemented games. Every image is an original generated SVG gameplay diagram; none is a rendered game screenshot or AI-painted concept image. All depicted networks and station names are fictional.

Going Up? 电梯奇旅

Elevator · tactical roguelike · Small–medium · strong first prototype · 10–15 minute runs

The elevator is your party, your inventory, and your getaway vehicle.

What the player actually does

At each stop, inspect three floor buttons, choose a destination, open the doors, and resolve a short encounter. Recruit a passenger, salvage a module, or retreat before the doors close. Reach the rooftop with your strange little crew.

Controls & decisions

Click a destination; drag passengers between six cabin spaces; play one passenger ability per turn. Encounters are turn-based, so this works on touch as well as desktop.

Why it is fun

People—not swords—make your build. A bellhop adds luggage storage. A botanist grows a vine into a secret floor. A sleepy giant grants strength but occupies two spaces. A ghost can pass through closed doors but refuses sunlit floors. Passenger friendships unlock combination abilities.

A memorable 30 seconds

The aquarium floor is flooding. Your botanist can seal the door, but the talking fish offers a shortcut if you carry its tank. Take the fish and lose a cabin slot, or keep space for the rooftop rescue?

Rules that make it fair

Weight is a visible resource, not a surprise punishment. Preview floor risks and offer a safe stop after difficult encounters. Losing a run unlocks new passenger stories; permanent unlocks add options rather than raw power.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

One 2D cabin, eight authored floor encounters, six passengers, three modules, and a ten-stop run. Recombine authored encounters with seeded selection; do not build a whole explorable tower or generate dialogue with AI.

Grow it later

Later: themed tower packs, daily seeds, passenger relationship collections, and asynchronous sharing of unusual cabin builds.

Escalator Orchestra 扶梯拼拼乐

Escalator · construction puzzle · Small · easiest elevator idea to ship · 2–5 minute puzzles

Fit moving stairways together, then watch a shopping mall turn into a little people machine.

What the player actually does

A piece queue supplies escalators, landing bridges, and junctions. Rotate and place them on a side-view grid to connect entrances with destination floors. Press Play to send a passenger wave through your creation; pause, repair, and replay.

Controls & decisions

Drag a piece, rotate with a button or keyboard, flip its travel direction, then run the simulation. Planning has no timer; an optional rush mode introduces a falling-piece countdown.

Why it is fun

Borrow the fitting-and-rotation appeal of falling-block puzzles, not the exact Tetris identity. Completing a useful route earns a new piece; floors do not disappear. Two passengers reaching different goals on the same beat create a musical combo. Carrying a balloon reveals overhead clearance problems.

A memorable 30 seconds

A red shopper needs floor 3, a blue shopper needs floor 2, and you have one long escalator left. A timed junction sends the first to the bridge and the second upward. The whole mall plays a satisfying two-note chord.

Rules that make it fair

Every escalator needs valid landings and clear space. Show direction arrows and predicted paths before Play. Add elevators or ramps for wheelchair routes; escalators are not treated as accessible substitutes.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

A 10-by-8 grid, two passenger destinations, six piece types, deterministic passenger routing, and twelve handcrafted puzzles. A route graph drives the simulation; animated steps are only visual.

Grow it later

Later: station interchanges, moving walkways, daily piece seeds, a sandbox, and shareable puzzles with a verified solution.

Shaft Sprint 升降冲刺

Elevator · arcade racing · Medium · timing needs playtesting · 90-second races

Race elevator cabins through a tower made of lanes, stops, and deliciously risky shortcuts.

What the player actually does

Accelerate up one of three shafts, brake precisely at transfer floors, slide through a connector, and choose your next shaft. Three short stages make a race. Optional delivery stops trade a few seconds for a useful upgrade.

Controls & decisions

Hold to accelerate, release to brake, and tap left/right at a lit transfer gate. The cabin follows constrained rails; you are racing decisions and timing, not wrestling a free-flying vehicle.

Why it is fun

Draft behind another cabin for a charge boost. Nail a smooth stop for a slingshot departure. Pick a turbo battery, a one-use express pass, or a spring connector. A catch-up battery gives trailing racers tactical options without stealing steering control.

A memorable 30 seconds

The leader takes the straight express shaft. You brake at the laundromat, pick up a delivery battery, and use a diagonal maintenance connector to emerge one floor ahead.

Rules that make it fair

Transfers work only at marked gates, so routes stay readable. Start with AI and ghost races; leave online multiplayer and complex physics for later. Treat it as an original arcade game, not a Mario Kart reskin.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

A side-on 2.5D tower, three shafts, one course, two AI opponents, three power-ups, and a time-trial ghost. Deterministic rail motion plus simple collision rules.

Grow it later

Later: course editor, local split-screen, rotating tower themes, and sideways connectors. Upside-down racing is a late novelty, not the core control scheme.

Last Train Home 末班车：送你回家

Metro · cozy supernatural mystery · Small–medium · writing-heavy, code-light · 20–30 minute episodes

Not every passenger is alive. Every passenger still deserves the right stop.

What the player actually does

Walk through a compact carriage, speak to passengers, inspect tickets and reflections, then choose a stop or transfer on the network map. Gather enough evidence to take each passenger home before the last connections close.

Controls & decisions

Click objects and dialogue choices; pin clues to a notebook; select a route. A relaxed mode pauses the timetable while reading. Use text, symbols, and visible clues—not audio alone.

Why it is fun

Supernatural behavior follows discoverable rules. A ghost ticket names a station that was renamed. A passenger appears only in the window reflection. A musician repeats the same verse whenever you ride a loop. Your job is to understand them, not randomly accuse or eject them.

A memorable 30 seconds

A child holds an old ticket to a renamed station. The physical station still exists, but an evening newspaper and a changing map let you connect its old name to the new one. The child vanishes gently after the correct announcement.

Rules that make it fair

Every solution needs at least two supporting clues. Ordinary passengers can be unusual too; strange behavior is not proof of danger. Missing a connection opens another route or ending instead of silently making the case impossible.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

One reusable carriage, six fictional stations, four authored mysteries, a clue notebook, and two endings. Reuse station backgrounds; do not recreate an entire city in 3D.

Grow it later

Real-city packs reuse actual station order and transfers while placing explicitly fictional stories on top. Historical names require research; ghost platforms are visibly separated from real service information.

Rush Hour Rescue 地铁调度室

Metro · real-world-inspired operations simulation · Medium–large · most ambitious systems idea · 10–20 minute scenarios

A train being on time is nice. A whole city making its connections is the real victory.

What the player actually does

Observe platform queues and train positions; add a spare train, adjust departures, briefly hold a connection, or turn a service back at an allowed terminal. Run the next few minutes and read the passenger consequences.

Controls & decisions

Click a train or station on a schematic map, choose from valid dispatch actions, and use pause or speed controls. A visible forecast compares likely queue changes before committing.

Why it is fun

Solve competing needs rather than simply buying more trains. Event crowds leave one station, an airport connection approaches closing time, and another platform is filling. A helpful hold can save a transfer while causing a queue elsewhere.

A memorable 30 seconds

A concert ends near a stadium. You use a spare train to clear its platform, then hold a connecting line for 20 seconds. The departing wave becomes a glowing ribbon of passengers making their last transfer.

Rules that make it fair

Enforce simplified safe separation and platform capacity as hard constraints; never reward reckless train operation. Score passenger waiting and crowding alongside service reliability. This is a game model, not a professional dispatch trainer.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

Two intersecting lines, twelve stations, one depot, aggregate passenger groups, and three scenarios. Use a discrete-event simulation and simplified dwell/headway rules, not individual commuter AI.

Grow it later

Import licensed real-city topology and schedules where available. Add city-specific scenarios, demand presets, service-change planning, and an optional deeper operations model only when data supports it.

One Ticket, Many Errands 一票通城

Metro · route-planning adventure · Small · best first real-city-capable game · 5–10 minute trips

Turn a familiar subway map into a city-sized puzzle of tiny acts of kindness.

What the player actually does

Accept three errands, inspect station facilities and deadlines, plan a route, then ride and decide whether to get off or continue. Finish with limited time, a small bag, and the fewest unnecessary transfers.

Controls & decisions

Click stations to queue a route, reorder errands, and choose actions at stops. Show predicted travel and transfer time, then reveal optional complications in a clearly labeled challenge mode.

Why it is fun

Deliver a cake without using the crowded peak service, return a lost umbrella, and catch a rooftop picnic before sunset. A fragile parcel prefers fewer transfers; a hot lunch wants the fastest arrival. Bonus clues lead you to a delightful station mural.

A memorable 30 seconds

Two errands point in opposite directions. Completing the umbrella delivery first unlocks a locker at the interchange, letting you stash the bulky parcel and take a faster loop to the picnic.

Rules that make it fair

Separate fictional errand deadlines from real timetables. Accessibility mode plans step-free paths only when the imported pack has validated accessibility information; otherwise label it unknown rather than assume.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

A fictional 15-station network, eight errands, three item constraints, fixed travel times, and illustrated stop vignettes. A shortest-path solver provides hints rather than always choosing for the player.

Grow it later

Best entry point for real-city packs: recognizable maps become playable with no detailed station models. Add local stories and landmarks from permitted sources; let players create purely fictional errands.

Transfer Garden 换乘花园

Metro · station-building simulation · Medium · naturally joins elevators and metro · 15–30 minute builds

Build an interchange that feels like a welcoming little garden, not a confusing maze.

What the player actually does

Place platforms, corridors, signs, escalators, elevators, and rest areas. Run a passenger wave, watch bottlenecks, then rearrange the station. Optimize different journeys rather than a single throughput number.

Controls & decisions

Build on a compact grid; rotate pieces; select a passenger group to see its route. Toggle a crowd heatmap, step-free network, and walking-time overlay.

Why it is fun

Different travelers expose different design problems. A family needs a meeting spot, a visitor follows signs, and a wheelchair user needs a continuous lift route. Clever placement makes streams separate and rejoin like a choreographed dance.

A memorable 30 seconds

A short escalator clears one queue but floods a narrow bridge. Widening the bridge is expensive. Moving a sign and routing exiting passengers around a garden solves the jam while keeping the step-free route intact.

Rules that make it fair

Show pedestrian capacities and valid connections. Emergency routes are constraints, not score exploits. Optimize walking time, crowding, accessibility, and comfort; the prettiest station may not be the best one.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

One fictional interchange, a 20-by-20 grid, six building tools, three traveler groups, and aggregate flow simulation. Do not simulate every person with expensive collision physics.

Grow it later

Use real-city line/platform relationships to inspire stations, but label layouts as fictional unless independently sourced. Combine with Escalator Orchestra as its longer-form sandbox mode.

The Missing Line 不存在的环线

Metro · supernatural map deduction · Small–medium · unusual and very map-friendly · 5–15 minute puzzles

An extra line appears on the map only after midnight. Discover its rules before it folds the city.

What the player actually does

Compare the normal network with a supernatural overlay; observe anomalous journeys; test a route hypothesis; mark which ghost connections are real. Rescue three stranded passengers using the rules you uncover.

Controls & decisions

Click connections to mark a theory, take a test ride, and inspect a journey log. The entire game can live on a schematic map with short station vignettes.

Why it is fun

Treat the haunting as a solvable logic system. A ghost edge works only after changing line twice; a mirrored station reverses route direction; a ring closes only when visited clockwise. Rules are revealed through controlled experiments, not random jump scares.

A memorable 30 seconds

Your journey log says A → B took two minutes, but B → A took you to C. Comparing two rides reveals that the phantom shuttle advances one station each time the normal circle line crosses midnight.

Rules that make it fair

The generator chooses from a small tested rule library and verifies that objectives are reachable. Every anomaly is visually marked; the normal city graph remains unchanged and can always be inspected.

Smallest worthwhile prototype

A ten-station fictional network, three authored anomaly rules, five puzzles, a hypothesis notebook, and one rescue objective per puzzle. Build a rule evaluator and reachability checker before procedural puzzles.

Grow it later

Put the same rule system over real-city topology. Circles, branches, and interchange clusters create different puzzle shapes. Use a fictional overlay disclaimer and avoid pretending that imaginary stations are official.

One city-map foundation. Several very different games.

Start with the city's network as a graph—not a geographically exact 3D city. This keeps expansion affordable and lets the same city pack power several games.

Separate the layers

  1. Physical places: stable station IDs, names, optional coordinates, and station complexes.
  2. Service network: line-specific stops, branches, directions, ride links, transfer links, service calendars, and travel times. Two lines sharing a station are not automatically an instant transfer.
  3. Presentation: original schematic positions, line styles, labels, language, and optional backgrounds.
  4. Gameplay overlay: errands, crowds, stories, and ghost connections. Never modify the underlying real network to make a fantasy puzzle.

Make missing data honest

Topology alone supports Courier and Missing Line. Dispatch also needs service patterns, travel times, dwell assumptions, and a demand model. Station building needs separate layout data: a network map cannot tell you where a real escalator is.

Prefer an operator's downloadable, licensed GTFS feed when available; availability and redistribution terms vary by city. GTFS can provide scheduled service but does not guarantee accurate indoor layouts, real-time crowding, or complete step-free routing. OpenStreetMap is another possible source, subject to its attribution and ODbL obligations.

Do not scrape a proprietary map image and assume it can be redistributed. Draw an original schematic from permitted data. Record the source, license, attribution, snapshot date, and all estimated values in each city pack.

Suggested import and validation pipeline

Licensed source → importer → canonical network → graph checks → original schematic → game overlay

  • Retain branches and direction-specific services; don't flatten a branched line into one station list.
  • Distinguish station complexes, platforms, and line-specific stops. Model walking transfers explicitly; keep walking time separate from waiting time.
  • Validate unique IDs, links, reachability, valid service calendars, and nonnegative travel times. Flag disconnected services rather than silently joining them.
  • Save a versioned offline snapshot. Mark it “Game data, not journey advice.” Do not imply live accuracy.
  • Keep curated city stories separate from imported operational data. Localize display names without changing IDs.
  • For supernatural packs, test puzzle reachability and show a switch between the real network and the fiction overlay.

Which cities first?

A fictional 15-station city first. Then choose one real city based on usable licensed data—not size or fame. Possible design inspirations: a compact loop for deduction; a radial network for timed errands; branches and crowded interchanges for dispatch. London, Tokyo, Shanghai, or your home city can all become packs, but each needs its own data and licensing check. No city's feed has been verified for this gallery.

What I would actually build first

Elevator winner: Escalator Orchestra

It has the shortest path to a visible, delightful toy: place a stairway, hit Play, and watch someone reach the right floor. First test just three floors, two destinations, and four pieces. Keep it if players voluntarily rearrange and replay their solutions.

Runner-up: Going Up? is more characterful, but it needs authored encounters and careful balancing. Shaft Sprint should wait until its braking/transfer mechanic feels good without power-ups.

Metro winner: One Ticket, Many Errands

It is the cheapest way to prove that real-city maps themselves are fun. First test ten stations and three competing errands. Keep it if players find an alternate route and can explain why it was clever.

Then add Last Train Home as a narrative mode over the same route model. Add The Missing Line as a puzzle overlay. Build Dispatch later; it is a separate simulation project, not a small feature toggle.

A shared creative universe—not one enormous game

Keep these as distinct modes or prototypes with shared map and route tooling. A courier can meet a passenger from Last Train Home; a station can contain a puzzle from Escalator Orchestra. Those connections give personality without forcing every system into the first release.

Design assumptions: small-team, browser-first prototypes; stylized 2D or restrained 2.5D; keyboard/mouse/touch; non-graphic supernatural stories. Complexity labels are relative, not development-time promises. Start single-player and offline, and offer relaxed modes before adding competitive pressure.
